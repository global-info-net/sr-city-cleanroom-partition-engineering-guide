The Sri City Cleanroom Partition Engineering Guide: Designing Controlled Environments for Modern Manufacturing

«A practical engineering and procurement guide for pharmaceutical, medical-device, electronics, biotechnology, food-processing and other manufacturing teams planning controlled environments around Sri City.»

A cleanroom is not simply a room with white walls.

The wall system, ceiling, doors, junctions, penetrations, airflow strategy, pressure relationships, lighting, HVAC interfaces and maintenance access all become part of one controlled environment.

That distinction matters when planning a new facility, expanding an existing production area or converting an industrial space into a controlled manufacturing environment.

For project managers and procurement teams researching cleanroom partitions in Sri City, the first question should not be:

«"Which panel is cheapest?"»

It should be:

«"What level of environmental control does the process require, and how should the building envelope support that requirement?"»

ISO 14644-1:2015 defines cleanroom air-cleanliness classification by airborne particle concentration. It does not, by itself, define the physical construction system required to achieve a particular application. citeturn0search0

ISO 14644-4:2022 approaches cleanrooms as a complete lifecycle process covering requirements, design, construction and start-up, rather than treating the partition system as an isolated product. citeturn0search2

1. What a Cleanroom Partition Actually Does

A cleanroom partition forms part of the physical envelope separating controlled areas from surrounding spaces.

Its responsibilities can include:

- Maintaining room separation
- Supporting cleanable surfaces
- Accommodating doors
- Integrating viewing panels
- Supporting ceiling interfaces
- Controlling air leakage
- Providing service penetrations
- Supporting pressure-zone separation
- Allowing appropriate maintenance
- Contributing to contamination-control objectives

The partition therefore has both a construction function and an environmental-control function.

2. Start With the Process

The process should determine the room.

Before choosing panels, establish:

What is manufactured?

What contaminants matter?

What temperature and humidity conditions are required?

What pressure relationships are required?

What cleaning chemicals are used?

What equipment will enter the room?

How frequently will the space be cleaned?

What maintenance access is necessary?

What classification or regulatory requirements apply?

This information should be converted into a user requirement specification or equivalent project brief.

3. Cleanroom Classification Is Not a Wall Specification

This distinction is important.

A cleanroom classification relates to controlled environmental performance. ISO 14644-1 specifically addresses airborne particle concentration for classification. citeturn0search0

It does not mean that:

«"ISO Class X automatically requires panel Y."»

The correct wall construction depends on the complete application.

Factors include:

- Process
- Cleaning regime
- Pressure
- HVAC design

- Humidity
- Material compatibility
- Impact requirements
- Fire requirements
- Maintenance
- Regulatory environment

6. Wall Panel Construction

Cleanroom wall panels may be constructed using different materials and core configurations.

The selection should consider:

- Surface finish
- Core properties
- Thickness
- Thermal requirements
- Fire performance
- Acoustic requirements
- Impact resistance
- Chemical resistance
- Cleaning requirements
- Structural behaviour

No single panel construction is automatically correct for every facility.

---

7. Sandwich Panels

Insulated sandwich panels are commonly considered for controlled environments because they can combine:

outer skin + insulated core + finished surface

The precise panel construction should be selected against the project's performance requirements.

Questions should include:

- What core is proposed?
- What are the panel dimensions?
- How are joints formed?
- How are panels supported?
- How are penetrations treated?
- What happens at corners?
- How are doors connected?
- How is the ceiling interface sealed?

The joint system can be just as important as the panel face.

---

8. Panel Surface Selection

The internal face needs to tolerate the actual cleaning environment.

Consider:

- Frequency of cleaning
- Cleaning chemicals
- Disinfectants
- Scrubbing
- Impact
- Abrasion
- Moisture
- Temperature

A visually attractive finish that cannot withstand the operating regime is not an appropriate cleanroom finish.

---

9. Smooth Surfaces

Smooth surfaces can simplify cleaning by reducing areas where contamination can accumulate.

The objective is to minimise:

- Unnecessary ledges
- Open joints
- Difficult corners
- Rough transitions
- Exposed voids

The design should be evaluated as a complete surface system rather than by looking only at individual panels.

---

10. Cleanroom Coving

Wall-to-floor and wall-to-wall transitions deserve attention.

Traditional sharp corners can be difficult to clean.

Appropriately designed coving can create smoother transitions.

But the detail must be compatible with:

- Floor system
- Wall system
- Cleaning method
- Sealants
- Movement
- Maintenance

A beautiful wall panel cannot compensate for poorly executed junctions.

---

11. Panel Joints

Panel joints are critical.

A joint can influence:

- Air leakage

- Sealed
- Inspected
- Maintained

16. Airlocks and Pass-Throughs

17. Pressure Differential

Cleanrooms often use pressure relationships between spaces as part of contamination-control strategies.

The required pressure relationship depends on the process and facility design.

The wall system needs sufficient integrity for the HVAC strategy to function as intended.

This is why partition procurement should happen alongside HVAC coordination.

18. HVAC and Partition Coordination

A cleanroom wall can be perfectly installed and still perform poorly as part of the overall system if:

- Duct penetrations are badly sealed
- Ceiling interfaces are incomplete
- Doors leak excessively
- Service openings are uncontrolled
- Cable penetrations are poorly detailed

The partition package should therefore be coordinated with:

HVAC

Fire protection

19. Service Penetrations

Every penetration creates an interface.

Examples include:

- Electrical cables
- Data cables
- Pipes
- Instrumentation
- Process lines
- Sensors
- HVAC components

Each opening should have an approved detail.

Avoid improvising penetrations during installation.

---

20. Why Penetration Details Matter

A cleanroom may have excellent panels but dozens of poorly sealed service penetrations.

Those openings can undermine the integrity of the enclosure.

A professional project therefore prepares penetration details before installation.

- Difficult cleaning

- Damaged surfaces
- Poor service access

47. Brownfield vs Greenfield Cleanrooms

Greenfield

A cleanroom is incorporated into a new facility.

Advantages may include:

- Better coordination
- Greater design freedom
- Planned utilities
- Easier spatial integration

Brownfield

An existing facility is converted or upgraded.

Challenges may include:

- Existing columns
- Limited ceiling height
- Existing HVAC
- Existing utilities
- Production downtime
- Restricted access

Brownfield projects require particularly careful site surveys.

61. Common Cleanroom Partition Mistakes

Mistake 1: Choosing by panel price

A panel is only one part of the system.

Mistake 2: Ignoring interfaces

Most problems appear where systems meet.

Mistake 3: Designing walls separately from HVAC

The two systems interact.

Mistake 4: Leaving penetrations until the end

Late penetrations create uncontrolled details.

Mistake 5: Forgetting maintenance

A cleanroom must remain maintainable after commissioning.

Mistake 6: Treating classification as a product specification

Cleanliness classification and construction selection are related but not interchangeable.
